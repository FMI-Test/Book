# A History of Error: 12 Debunked Paleoanthropological Theories

## 12 Theories on How We Became Human and Why They Are All Wrong

Published January 24, 2021

Humans are unusual animals, but the hard question is not whether we are unusual. The harder question is what, exactly, made us so. Over the last century, scientists and science writers have proposed many answers. Most of those theories capture something real, but each one also reflects the assumptions of its own era. The problem begins when any single trait is treated as the decisive moment that made us human.

1. **We make tools:** [Kenneth Oakley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Oakley) argued that shaping tools for a specific purpose was the first clearly human behavior. That view weakened once [Jane Goodall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Goodall) and others showed that chimpanzees also modify sticks and use them as tools. Tool use matters, but it is not uniquely ours.

2. **We are killers:** [Raymond Dart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_Dart) proposed that our ancestors became human through predation, violence, and aggressive dominance. His killer ape theory resonated in the aftermath of World War II, but it now reads more like a projection of modern anxieties than a complete explanation of prehistory.

3. **We share food:** [Glynn Isaac](https://www.stoneageinstitute.org/glynn-isaac.html) argued that food sharing changed everything. Once carcasses and gathered resources were brought back to a central place, early humans had to cooperate, communicate, and maintain more stable social bonds. That is persuasive, but food sharing alone does not explain the full package of human behavior.

4. **We swim naked:** [Elaine Morgan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elaine_Morgan) popularized the aquatic ape hypothesis, claiming that hairlessness, upright posture, and other human traits emerged because our ancestors adapted to a waterside environment. The theory remains famous, but most anthropologists do not consider the evidence strong enough to support it.

5. **We throw things:** Archaeologist Reid Ferring connected human emergence to the ability to hurl stones at speed and in groups. At [Dmanisi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dmanisi_hominins), a 1.8-million-year-old hominin site in present-day Georgia, the argument is that coordinated stone throwing may have helped small-bodied hominins defend carcasses and survive among large predators. Throwing likely mattered, but it was not the only turning point.

6. **We hunt:** [Sherwood Washburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherwood_Washburn) and C. S. Lancaster argued that hunting produced cooperation, planning, memory, and division of labor. Hunting surely influenced human evolution, but theories that make it the master key often struggle to explain the equally important roles of gathering, childcare, and social flexibility.

7. **We trade food for sex:** In C. Owen Lovejoy's provisioning model, monogamy and food sharing between males and females reshaped human evolution. Upright walking freed the hands, provisioning encouraged pair bonds, and pair bonds supported child-rearing. It is an elegant theory, but it still narrows a long, branching process into one behavioral bargain.

8. **We eat cooked food:** [Richard Wrangham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Wrangham) argued that cooking transformed human biology by making food easier to digest and freeing up more energy for larger brains. Cooking is a powerful explanation for later human development, but it still does not capture the whole story by itself.

9. **We eat cooked carbohydrates:** Another view is that cooked tubers and starch-rich plants, not just meat, helped fuel brain growth. Amylase lets the body turn starch into glucose, and research on amylase gene copy number suggests that starch processing became increasingly important in human evolution. That broadens the diet story, but it still does not reduce humanity to one nutritional breakthrough.

10. **We walk on two legs:** The classic savanna hypothesis says bipedalism emerged when drying climates pushed our ancestors into open landscapes where standing upright improved travel and visibility. But fossils such as _Ardipithecus ramidus_ complicate that story because upright walking appears to predate fully open savanna conditions. Bipedalism was crucial, but it was not a single clean switch.

11. **We adapt:** [Rick Potts](https://humanorigins.si.edu/research/hop-team/rick-potts) argues that human evolution was shaped not by one stable environment but by repeated environmental instability. In this view, flexibility itself became the defining human advantage. This is one of the strongest frameworks, but even adaptability is still part of a larger mosaic rather than the whole answer.

12. **We unite and conquer:** [Curtis Marean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtis_Marean) emphasizes cooperation, innovation, and group-level advantage. Humans spread across the planet not just because individuals were clever, but because groups could coordinate, share risk, and outcompete rivals. That explains a great deal, but even deep cooperation emerged alongside many other changes rather than replacing them.

## What Is Wrong with These Theories?

Most of them are partly right. What they get wrong is the assumption that humanity can be reduced to one decisive feature, one ecological shift, or one behavioral revolution.

Our ancestors were not moving toward a predetermined finish line. They were surviving under changing conditions as different kinds of hominins, including _Homo erectus_ and other early humans. Tool use, food sharing, throwing, hunting, cooking, starch consumption, bipedalism, adaptability, and cooperation all mattered. None of them alone explains why we became what we are.
